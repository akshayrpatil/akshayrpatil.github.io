The part of the order nobody designed

Pickily was Udaan's quick-commerce grocery app. Most of the product energy went where you'd expect — catalogue, search, cart, checkout. Everything after "order placed" had been built incrementally, screen by screen, by whoever needed it that quarter.

The result was four features that behaved like four different products: a tracking screen, an order list, a cancellation flow, and a refund status page. Each one had its own vocabulary for the same order, its own idea of what a "state" was, and its own dead end when something went wrong.

I worked end-to-end on this — mapping the states with Product and Ops, redesigning the flows, and working with Engineering through a phased rollout.

What I owned

Reading the support queue instead of the roadmap

The fastest way into this problem wasn't a usability test — it was the customer support queue. I sat with CS tickets and call reasons for the post-order window and sorted them by what the user was actually trying to find out.

Almost none of the calls were complaints about the grocery order. They were requests for information the app already had, but hadn't shown: where the order was, whether the cancellation had worked, and when the money was coming back.

The clearest signal was the support queue going quiet. The second — the one I didn't expect to be arguing about — was infrastructure cost.

The maps saving came from a design decision, not an engineering one. The old tracking screen kept a live map polling continuously for the entire order lifetime, including the long stretch where the order was still being packed and there was nothing to show. Replacing that stretch with a status timeline — and only mounting the live map once a rider was actually assigned — cut the API calls without taking anything away from the user.

What I learned

Post-order is where trust is either earned or lost, and it's almost always the least-designed part of a commerce app. Nobody demos it. Everybody calls support about it.

I also learned that "the user doesn't know what's happening" is usually not a content problem — it's a states problem. Once Product, Ops, Engineering and I were describing an order with the same words, the copy stopped being hard to write.

And a live map is a great way to say "we're on it" and an expensive way to say nothing. Knowing which moment needs which is the actual design work.
